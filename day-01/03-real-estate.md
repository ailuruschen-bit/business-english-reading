## Passage A — Property Listing

**HARTLEY & FINCH — Prestige Properties**

**17 Ashgrove Lane — The Old Bottling Works**
*A warehouse conversion for the discerning buyer*

They don't come much more sophisticated than this. Set on a gently sloping half-acre block a short walk from the village centre, this tastefully modernized warehouse conversion offers over 320 square metres of living space across two levels.

The original brickwork and cast-iron columns have been retained throughout. The kitchen has been rebuilt to a professional standard, with more **bench-space** than most restaurants and an adjoining pantry. All four bedrooms have **en-suite** bathrooms; the principal suite opens onto a private terrace.

At the rear, a separate studio — currently used as a home office — would equally suit a growing family in need of guest **accommodations**.

Properties of this calibre rarely come to market. At $1.24 million this is no **bargain**, but for a buyer seeking an **upscale residence** with genuine character, it represents exceptional value.

Viewings strictly by appointment. All **inquiries** to Ellen Hartley, (02) 5541 8820.

## Passage B — Column: "Buying Your First Home"

**Before You Sign: Three Things First-Time Buyers Overlook**
*by Marcus Oyelaran, Regional Real Estate Institute*

Most first-time buyers spend months comparing **properties** and almost no time comparing loans. That is a mistake. Last month Royal Bank lowered all its key **mortgage rates** **across the board**, **while** TD Canada Trust **excluded** its ten-year rate from the reduction. A borrower who failed to notice that difference could pay tens of thousands more over the life of the loan.

Second, buyers underestimate the **formalities**. Applicants should seek advice early and complete the formalities required to obtain a mortgage **well before** making an offer — a seller will almost always prefer a buyer whose financing is already arranged.

Third, remember that a house is not an investment you can sell in an afternoon. Buyers who have not yet **financially established themselves** should be cautious about stretching to the limit of what a lender will approve. By following a disciplined strategy, a couple I advised over fifteen years managed to **amass a small fortune** — but they did it by buying less house than they could afford, not more.

A final note on language: the flat itself may be **for sale**, but the furniture inside is not necessarily **on sale**.

## Notes

### 1. `property` / `real estate` / `premises` — not interchangeable

Your deck flags this trio, and it underpins every property passage in TOEIC:

| Word | What it refers to | Countability | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| `property` | ① an individual building or estate ② possessions in general | both | We have three **properties** for sale. / All equipment must remain council **property**. |
| `real estate` | **the sector**, land and buildings as a category | uncountable | a career in **real estate** |
| `premises` | **the whole site occupied by a business** | **always plural** | No smoking on the **premises**. |

Hooks:
- `premises` lives in rules and notices. When you see `on the premises`, read it as *inside this building or on these grounds*.
- `real estate` never takes a number: <s>three real estates</s> ❌

⚠️ In Passage B, `All equipment must remain council property` uses the **uncountable "possessions" sense** — the same sentence pattern as the exhibition rule in Unit 02. One word, two senses, both testable in the same paper.

### 2. `for sale` vs. `on sale` — one word apart, worlds apart

> The apartment is **for sale**, but the furniture inside is not **on sale**.

| Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `for sale` | **Available to buy** (discounted or not) |
| `on sale` | ① **Reduced in price** (American — the tested sense) ② released for purchase (British) |

So that sentence reads: *the flat is being sold, but the furniture is not discounted.* TOEIC uses **American** conventions: `on sale` = on discount.

Related, and also in your deck: `All sales are final.` = **no refunds or exchanges** — a fixture of retail small print.

### 3. `discerning` / `sophisticated` / `upscale` / `prestige` — the luxury adjective set

High-end advertising runs on a closed vocabulary. Every one of these words is saying the same thing: *this is expensive, and people of taste buy it.*

| Word | Literal | What it does in an ad |
|---|---|---|
| `discerning` | able to judge well | **of refined taste** (of the buyer) |
| `sophisticated` | complex, refined | **elegant, high-end** |
| `upscale` | upward in scale | **upmarket** (British: *upmarket*) |
| `prestige` / `prestigious` | standing, renown | **elite** |
| `exclusive` | excluding others | **select, high-class** — not "excluded"! |
| `tastefully modernized` | modernised with taste | **renovated with style**, not merely refitted |
| `of this calibre` | of this bore | **of this standard** |

⚠️ **`exclusive` deserves special attention** (it is in your deck). In advertising it means upmarket or restricted; as a verb in contracts it means leaving something out.
- `an exclusive residential district` = an upmarket area
- `This offer is exclusive to members.` = members only
- `Complete dinners are **excluded**.` = not included ← the verb

### 4. Housing vocabulary, settled once

- `warehouse conversion` = **a former warehouse turned into housing** (a loft). `conversion` = a change of use.
- `Victorian terrace` = **a Victorian-era terraced house**, one unit in a connected row. ⚠️ `terrace` also means a patio — Passage A uses that second sense in "opens onto a private terrace." Context decides.
- `mansion` = **a large, grand house**. ⚠️ Not an apartment. The Japanese loanword *manshon*, borrowed from this word, means a block of flats — the opposite end of the scale. If that association is in your head, mark it as a false friend.
- `en-suite bathroom` = **a bathroom attached directly to a bedroom** (French *en suite* = as a set). Listings abbreviate: *4 bed, 4 ensuite*.
- `block` = **a plot of land** (Australian and British usage). `a half-acre block` = half an acre of land.
- `gently sloping` = **on a mild slope** — advertising hedging, since sloping land is harder to sell.
- `bench-space` = **kitchen worktop space** (Australian; American English says *counter* or *countertop*).

### 5. `They don't come much more sophisticated than this.`

An English advertising idiom: literally *they do not come much more sophisticated than this one*, meaning **this is about as fine as they get**.

Same family:
- `They don't make them like this anymore.` = the old quality is gone
- `You won't find a better deal.` = this is the best available

These sentences are **inference-question bait** in Part 7: "What does the writer suggest about the property?" → always *It is of unusually high quality.*

### 6. `bargain` — the noun is the tested item

As a noun, `bargain` = **something sold cheaply, a good deal** — not haggling (that is the verb, `bargain with sb`).

> At $1.24 million this is no **bargain**.
> At that price, it is hardly cheap.

⚠️ That sentence is **negative**. Part 7 will ask "What does the agent admit about the price?" → *It is expensive.* The agent concedes the price and then pivots with "it represents exceptional value" — the classic advertising structure: concede, then justify.

`value` vs `price` vs `cost`:
- `price` = what is charged | `cost` = what you spend | `value` = what it is worth
- `represents exceptional value` = it is worth more than it costs

### 7. `inquiry` / `take out an ad` / `formalities`

- **`inquiry`** (also spelled `enquiry`) = a question or request for information.
  - Verb: `inquire **about** sth` (ask about) / `inquire **into** sth` (investigate)
  > Please **inquire about** the delivery status before contacting the supplier.
  - Plural `inquiries`; ads close with `All inquiries to…`

- **`take out an ad`** = **to place an advertisement** (to buy the space).
  `take out` here means to purchase or arrange: `take out insurance`, `take out a loan`, `take out a subscription`. Nothing to do with removing anything — learn this sense separately.

- **`formalities`** (usually plural) = **the required official procedures**.
  > complete the **formalities** required to obtain a mortgage
  Near-synonyms: `paperwork` (more colloquial), `procedures`.

### 8. `across the board` / `except for` / `while` — three words that decide comprehension

The opening of Passage B compresses a whole cluster of your banking cards, and what it really tests is **scope logic**:

> Royal Bank lowered all its key mortgage rates **across the board**, **while** TD Canada Trust **excluded** its ten-year rate.

- `across the board` = **applying to everything equally** (from the odds board at a racetrack).
- `while` here means **whereas**, not *when* — it marks **contrast**. This is a favourite TOEIC trap: when `while` sits mid-sentence with two different subjects on either side, it is almost always contrast.
- `except for` = **apart from** — carving one item out of a set.

So: **Royal cut everything; TD cut everything except the ten-year rate.** Part 7 asks "Which rate was NOT reduced by TD?" → the ten-year rate.

A related card: `rates on mortgages with **terms of four years or longer**`. ⚠️ In finance `term` means **duration**, not a technical word and not a clause (contract clauses are `terms and conditions`, plural).

### 9. `financially establish oneself` / `amass a fortune` / `financially sound`

- `financially establish oneself` = **to become financially secure**
- `amass a small fortune` = **to accumulate a substantial sum** (`amass` = to pile up; *a small fortune* is ironic and means a lot of money)
- `financially sound` = **in solid financial health** — `sound` as an adjective means healthy or reliable, nothing to do with noise
  > a financially **sound** company
- `net worth` = **total assets minus liabilities**
- `investment portfolio` = **the collection of assets someone holds** (*portfolio* originally a briefcase → everything carried in it)

## Self-test

**1.** Choose: Smoking is prohibited anywhere on the ______.
(A) real estate  (B) premises  (C) property line  (D) estate agent

<details><summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**(B) premises**. `on the premises` is the fixed way to say *anywhere on this site*, and it is always plural.

`real estate` names the sector and cannot refer to one specific site.

</details>

**2.** True or false: "The furniture is not on sale" means the furniture is not being sold.

<details><summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**False**, in the American usage TOEIC follows. `on sale` = **discounted**, so the sentence means *the furniture is not reduced in price*.

"Not being sold" would be `not for sale`. This one-word difference appears in advertising passages constantly.

</details>

**3.** Choose: The company remains financially ______ despite the downturn.
(A) sounded  (B) sound  (C) soundly  (D) sounding

<details><summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**(B) sound**. `financially sound` = in good financial health; `sound` is an **adjective** meaning robust.

The adverb `financially` modifies the adjective `sound`, and the whole phrase is the complement of `remains`. Choosing (C) would leave the adverb with nothing to modify.

</details>

**4.** According to Passage B, when should a first-time buyer complete their mortgage paperwork?

<details><summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Well before making an offer.**

Original: `complete the formalities required to obtain a mortgage **well before** making an offer`.

`well before` = considerably earlier than. That intensifying `well` (as in *well over*, *well within*, *well above*) is itself a frequent Part 5 item.

</details>

**5.** In Passage A: "At $1.24 million this is no bargain, but … it represents exceptional value." What is the agent doing?

<details><summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Conceding that the price is high, then arguing it is worth it.**

This concede-then-justify structure is standard in luxury copy. Part 7 asks "What does Hartley acknowledge about the property?" → *It is costly.*

The one thing that must not slip is `no bargain` versus `a bargain` — a single word reverses the sentence. **When reading advertisements, locate every negative first.** It is the cheapest error-prevention habit there is.

</details>
