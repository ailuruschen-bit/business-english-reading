## Warm-up: one passage containing every point in this unit

Read it once. If it is heavy going, that is expected — come back to it after the notes and it should run smoothly.

> **Please be advised that**, **despite** the delay announced last month, the Ashworth renovation will be completed on schedule. **Although** three subcontractors withdrew in August, the general contractor has replaced them, and work has continued for twenty **consecutive** days without interruption.
>
> **Since announcing** the project in March, the Council has held four public meetings. **Had the weather been** more favourable in September, the exterior work **would have been finished** already; **as it is**, it will be completed in April, **as was first planned**.
>
> Output **is predicted to increase** by eight percent next year. The figure **was forecast** by the Council's own analysts and **was broadcast** on local radio in October. **Even though** some residents remain sceptical, the Chair has said she will proceed **even if** the sceptics are proved right, because the Council **sees fit to** complete what it began.
>
> **Should you need** further information, please contact the Parks Officer. Requests **made orally** cannot be processed; the Chair cannot approve them **himself** without written confirmation.

## Notes

### 1. `despite` vs. `although` — what follows decides which

One of the most frequently tested points in Part 5, and the rule is a single line:

| Word | Part of speech | Takes |
|---|---|---|
| `despite` / `in spite of` | **preposition** | **a noun or gerund** (never a clause) |
| `although` / `though` / `even though` | **conjunction** | **a full clause** (subject + verb) |

```
despite  + the delay                    ✅ noun
despite  + being delayed                ✅ gerund
despite  + the fact that it was delayed ✅ noun + appositive clause (the universal converter)
despite  + it was delayed               ❌

although + it was delayed               ✅ full clause
although + the delay                    ❌
```

⚠️ Two frequent errors:
1. <s>despite **of** the delay</s> ❌ — `despite` already contains the sense of *of*. The version that takes *of* is `in spite **of**`.
2. **Never use two concessive markers at once**: <s>Although it rained, but we went.</s> ❌ English keeps one or the other, not both.

**The conversion trick** (most useful under time pressure):
- `Although he was ill, he came.` = `Despite **his illness**, he came.` = `Despite **being ill**, he came.`
- Any clause can be converted with `despite the fact that + clause` — the safe fallback.

### 2. `even if` vs. `even though` — hypothesis versus fact

The pair your deck names. The difference is **whether the speaker treats it as true**:

| Phrase | Meaning | Factual? |
|---|---|---|
| `even though` | **although (and it is so)** | ✅ already true |
| `even if` | **even supposing** | ❓ hypothetical |

> **Even though** it is raining, I'll go. — it **is** raining
> **Even if** it rains, I'll go. — it may or may not rain

In the warm-up:
- `**Even though** some residents remain sceptical` — they really are sceptical (fact)
- `she will proceed **even if** the sceptics are proved right` — supposing they turn out to be right (hypothesis)

**Hook**: `if` means *if*, so the one containing `if` is the hypothetical one.

### 3. The three faces of `while`

`while` is among the slipperiest words in TOEIC, and all three senses are tested:

| Use | Meaning | Clue |
|---|---|---|
| Time | during the time that | both sides describe **simultaneous** actions |
| **Contrast** | **whereas** | **two different subjects** doing opposing things |
| Concession | although (formal) | usually **sentence-initial**, with a turn in the main clause |

> Royal Bank lowered all its rates, **while** TD excluded its 10-year rate.
> → **contrast**: two banks, two behaviours. Not "at the time when TD excluded…"

> **While** the plan is ambitious, it is not unrealistic.
> → **concession**: although…

**Rule of thumb**: if the two sides carry **different subjects doing contrasting things**, read it as *whereas*; if the two sides are two aspects of one thing, read it as *although*.

### 4. `as was planned` — `as` as a relative pronoun

Your deck marks this three separate times, so it is a genuine sticking point. Here it is in full.

`as` here is a **relative pronoun** standing for **the whole main clause**, and it functions as the **subject** (or object) of its own clause.

```
The meeting will be held on Friday, as was planned.
                                    └─ as = "holding the meeting on Friday"
                                       and it is the subject of "was planned"
```

So **no `it` may be added**:

| Form | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| `as was planned` | ✅ | *as* is already the subject |
| `as planned` | ✅ | reduced; the most natural |
| `as was first planned` | ✅ | an adverb changes nothing |
| `as it was planned` | ❌ | *as* and *it* compete for one slot |
| `as we had planned` | ✅ | here *as* is the **object** (*we had planned **it***), so a subject is needed |

Other fixed expressions with relative `as`, all frequent:
- `as is often the case`
- `as was expected`
- `as is shown in the chart`
- `as mentioned above`
- `as it is` — **as things actually stand** (a different, idiomatic item; see the warm-up)

### 5. Inversion with `if` omitted — the mark of formal writing

TOEIC business correspondence rarely writes `if`. It **inverts** instead. Three cases, and only three:

| Full form | Inverted | Use |
|---|---|---|
| `If you **should** need help, …` | **`Should you need`** help, … | a future possibility (most common) |
| `If it **had been** possible, …` | **`Had it been`** possible, … | contrary to past fact |
| `If I **were** you, …` | **`Were I`** you, … | contrary to present fact |

**The rule**: move `should / had / were` in front of the subject and delete `if`. **No other verb inverts this way.**

The formulas worth memorising whole:
- **`Should you need further assistance, please contact us.`**
- **`Should there be any changes, we will notify you promptly.`**
- **`Should you have any questions, feel free to call.`**

⚠️ Note `Should there be…` — the inverted form of a *there be* clause, and very common.

### 6. Counterfactuals: contrary to past fact

The complete pair from your deck:

> **If** the circumstances **had been** more favorable, John **would have performed** a great deal better.
> **Inverted**: **Had** the circumstances been more favorable, John would have performed a great deal better.

**The pattern**:
```
If + subject + had + past participle ,  subject + would/could/might + have + past participle
     └── condition (contrary to past fact)   └── consequence (equally unreal)
```

**What it means**: the circumstances were **not** favourable, and John did **not** perform well.

Part 7 draws inferences from exactly this: "What does the statement suggest about John's performance?" → *It was disappointing.*

The other two conditional types, for completeness:

| Type | *if* clause | Main clause |
|---|---|---|
| Contrary to **past** fact | had + past participle | would **have** + past participle |
| Contrary to **present** fact | past tense (*were* for *be*) | would + base verb |
| Unlikely **future** | were to do / should do | would + base verb |

### 7. `Since announcing…` — participles must match the subject

> **Since announcing** his retirement, Mr. Kinsley has been busy training his replacement.

`Since announcing` is **preposition + gerund**, and its implied subject must be the subject of the main clause:

```
Since announcing his retirement,  Mr. Kinsley has been busy…
      └─ who announced? ───────────→ Mr. Kinsley ✅ they match
```

⚠️ **The dangling modifier** is the classic failure here:
- <s>Since announcing his retirement, the company has hired a replacement.</s> ❌
  (The company did not announce *his* retirement — the implied subjects do not match.)

Note the tense too: `Since + a point in time` forces the main clause into a **perfect tense** (*has been busy*). This is not optional.
- `since 2019`, `since announcing`, `since the merger` → `have/has + past participle`

### 8. `finite` vs. `non-finite clauses` — the terms in your deck

Understanding these two clears an entire question type at once:

- **`finite`**: the verb **carries tense and agreement** and can serve as a clause's main verb.
  > He **announces** / He **announced** — tense is marked → finite
- **`non-finite`**: the verb **has no tense**, and takes one of three shapes:
  - infinitive `to announce`
  - gerund or present participle `announcing`
  - past participle `announced`

**Why it matters**: Part 5 verb-form questions are really asking *does this blank have to carry the sentence's predicate?*

```
Method: find the verb that is already the predicate.
  → one already exists → the blank must be non-finite (to do / doing / done)
  → none exists yet    → the blank must be finite (a tensed form)
```

Worked example:
> The report, ______ by the committee last week, recommends three changes.
> (A) approved  (B) approves  (C) approving  (D) to approve

The predicate `recommends` is already there, so the blank must be non-finite. The report is *approved by* the committee, so the past participle: **(A) approved**.

### 9. `himself` vs. `by himself` — the difference a *by* makes

Another pair from your deck:

| Form | Meaning | Position |
|---|---|---|
| `himself` (emphatic) | **personally, in person** (him and no one else) | right after the subject, or at the end |
| `by himself` | **alone**, or **unaided** | at the end |

> The Chair cannot approve them **himself**. — not even the Chair personally can
> He finished the report **by himself**. — with no help

⚠️ A third use is the reflexive **object**: `He hurt **himself**.` — the action returns to the doer. That one never takes *by*.

### 10. Irregular verbs: `forecast` and `broadcast`

Your deck singles these out because **all three forms are identical**:

| Base | Past | Past participle |
|---|---|---|
| forecast | **forecast** | **forecast** |
| broadcast | **broadcast** | **broadcast** |
| cast | cast | cast |
| cost | cost | cost |
| set / put / cut / hit / let / shut / spread / burst | unchanged | unchanged |

> The figure **was forecast** by analysts and **was broadcast** on local radio.

⚠️ `forecasted` and `broadcasted` do occur in modern usage, but **TOEIC tests the invariable forms**. If both appear among the options, choose `forecast`.

### 11. `will increase` vs. `is predicted to increase` — degrees of certainty

Your card here is really about **hedging**, and Part 6 relies on it:

| Expression | Certainty |
|---|---|
| `Output **will** increase.` | an assertion |
| `Output **is expected to** increase.` | expectation |
| `Output **is predicted to** increase.` | forecast, evidence-based |
| `Output **is likely to** increase.` | probable |
| `Output **may** increase.` | possible |

**Why this is tested**: business documents — especially financial reports and forecasts — **do not assert what has not happened**. If the preceding sentence says "analysts estimate…", the blank wants `is predicted to`, not `will`.

**The reverse also holds**: a notice announcing a fixed arrangement uses `will` (*The store will open at nine*), because that is a scheduled fact, not a prediction.

### 12. Four small but frequent items

- **`consecutive`** = **successive, unbroken**
  > for seven **consecutive** days
  = `for seven days in a row` = `for seven straight days`
  ⚠️ Not `consequent` (following as a result).
- **`see fit to do sth`** = **to consider it appropriate to do** (formal, with a note of exercising one's own judgement)
  > The Council **sees fit to** complete what it began.
  Also `as you see fit` = as you judge best.
- **`Please be advised that…`** = **please note** — the opening formula of a formal notice, generally followed by a rule or an unwelcome consequence.
- **`made orally`** = **stated in speech rather than writing**
  > Requests **made orally** cannot be processed.
  - `orally` = in speech | `in writing` = in written form | `verbally` (loosely, in speech)
  - `made orally` is a **past-participle phrase postmodifying the noun**: *requests [that are] made orally*
  - ⚠️ This is the finite/non-finite test in action: the predicate is `cannot be processed`, so `made` can only be non-finite.

## Self-test (twelve items — finish all of them before checking)

**1.** ______ the heavy rain, the outdoor ceremony proceeded as planned.
(A) Although  (B) Despite  (C) Even though  (D) While

**2.** ______ the forecast is accurate, we will still need a contingency plan.
(A) Even if  (B) Even though  (C) Despite  (D) In spite of

**3.** The northern branch exceeded its target, ______ the southern branch fell short.
(A) while  (B) despite  (C) unless  (D) so that

**4.** The conference will be held in Boston, ______ .
(A) as it was planned  (B) as was planned  (C) as planning  (D) as been planned

**5.** ______ you require additional documentation, please contact our office.
(A) Would  (B) Should  (C) Could  (D) Might

**6.** ______ the equipment been serviced on time, the breakdown could have been avoided.
(A) If  (B) Should  (C) Had  (D) Were

**7.** ______ his retirement in May, Mr. Kinsley has trained two successors.
(A) Since announcing  (B) Since he announcing  (C) Since announced  (D) Since to announce

**8.** The revised figures ______ by the ministry last Thursday.
(A) were broadcasting  (B) were broadcast  (C) have broadcast  (D) broadcasting

**9.** Analysts say demand ______ to rise by six percent next year.
(A) will  (B) is predicted  (C) predicts  (D) predicting

**10.** The plant operated for eleven ______ days without a single stoppage.
(A) consequent  (B) consecutive  (C) considerable  (D) consistent

**11.** The director reviewed every application ______ before making a decision.
(A) himself  (B) his own  (C) by his  (D) him

**12.** The proposal, ______ by the committee in March, is now before the board.
(A) approves  (B) approving  (C) approved  (D) approval

<details><summary><b>Answers and explanations (open after finishing)</b></summary>

**1. (B) Despite** — what follows is the noun phrase `the heavy rain`, so a **preposition** is needed. (A) and (C) are conjunctions; (D) as a concessive also requires a clause.

**2. (A) Even if** — "supposing the forecast proves accurate" is **hypothetical**, and the main clause says a backup is needed regardless. (B) would assert that the forecast *is* accurate, which contradicts the sentence. (C) and (D) are prepositions and cannot take a clause.

**3. (A) while** — two different subjects (northern branch, southern branch) doing opposite things → the **contrast** sense, *whereas*.

**4. (B) as was planned** — `as` is a relative pronoun serving as the subject of its clause, so no `it`. (C) and (D) are structurally impossible. `as planned` would also be correct but is not offered.

**5. (B) Should** — the inverted conditional `Should you require…` = *If you should require…*. The other modals cannot invert this way, and only *should*, *had* and *were* can head an if-less conditional.

**6. (C) Had** — the main clause is `could **have been** avoided` (contrary to past fact), so the condition must be `had been serviced`, inverted to `Had the equipment been serviced`.

**7. (A) Since announcing** — `since` takes a gerund whose implied subject matches the main subject, Mr. Kinsley. Note the main clause is in the present perfect, `has trained`, which `since` requires.

**8. (B) were broadcast** — all three forms of *broadcast* are identical, so the passive is `were broadcast`. (A) mixes progressive and passive incoherently.

**9. (B) is predicted** — `demand is predicted to rise`. Analysts forecast rather than assert, so `will` is wrong in register; and since `to rise` is already given, the blank needs a structure that takes an infinitive.

**10. (B) consecutive** = successive. (A) *consequent* = resulting; (C) *considerable* = large; (D) *consistent* = steady, but it does not describe an unbroken run of days.

**11. (A) himself** — the emphatic reflexive, "in person," placed after the object or at the end. (B) cannot stand alone adverbially; (C) and (D) are ungrammatical.

**12. (C) approved** — the predicate `is` already exists, so the blank must be **non-finite**; the proposal was *approved by* the committee, so the past participle serves as a postmodifier. This question is a direct application of the method in section 8.
